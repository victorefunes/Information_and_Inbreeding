\begin{abstract}
    \noindent\singlespacing
    The United States supplies nearly half of the world's traded dairy bull genetics. Since 2008, genomic selection, an information technology that lets breeders read an animal's genetic merit at birth, has more than doubled the pace of genetic improvement. At the same time, the entire market saw a marked increase in inbreeding because of companies selecting more from a small pool of ``elite'' bulls.
    We hypothesize that, because genomic testing substantially increased the number of bulls on the market, companies chose already popular bulls to distinguish themselves to producers.
    We test this using National Association of Animal Breeders data on Holstein bulls marketed from 2001 to 2020, comparing popular lines with propensity-score-matched controls of comparable genetic merit in a difference-in-differences design. After 2010, inbreeding in popular lines rose by 1.2-3.5 percentage points relative to controls (about 50 percent), with no differential pre-trend, and the result holds under alternative matching estimators. The problem of inbreeding in dairy cattle appears to bear some similarities to other coordination issues in economics, and future policy ideas to slow inbreeding may benefit from considering that the full costs of inbreeding are not currently captured by breeders.

    \vspace{1em}
    \noindent\textbf{Keywords:} genomic selection; inbreeding; coordination failure; dairy cattle genetics
    \noindent\textbf{JEL codes:} Q16, O33, D62, Q13, L13
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction\label{sec:intro}}
The US is one of the world's largest suppliers of dairy bull genetics.
In 2022, the United States alone exported \$295 million of bovine semen, representing 47\% of the world's exports in value terms (Figure \ref{fig:semen_exp}). For about six decades, milk production per cow has consistently increased in the US herd, and more than half of this growth is estimated to be attributable to improvements in cattle genetics \citep{cdcbimpact}. Thus, US breeders' efforts have likely had substantial impacts on the global dairy industry.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=.8\textwidth]{figures/semen_exports.png}
    \caption{Share of trade Value of Bovine Semen exports by country (2019)\\ \small \textbf{Source}: UN Comtrade database}
    \label{fig:semen_exp} 
\end{figure}

In the modern dairy industry, dairy breeders improve cow milk yield by identifying bulls with the most productive female offspring and then selling their semen to dairy farmers looking to improve their genetics. The speed of this genetic improvement has previously been limited by biological factors. Determining whether a bull had good milk production ability took at least five years since the bull had to have offspring born from its use on dairy farms, and those offspring would need to take two years to grow to production age.

However, these limits became less binding after the advent of a technological innovation: genomic testing. Introduced in 2008, this technological innovation allows breeders to link each animal's genes to productive outcomes such as milk yield and fat yield. The practical upshot is that breeders can now determine a dairy bull's quality at birth rather than at 5 years. Genomic testing has two important, documented impacts on the dairy bull market. First, the pace of genetic improvement in dairy cattle has more than doubled because breeders can identify productive bulls at much younger ages and breed new generations more quickly. Second, dairy companies have more than doubled the number of bulls for sale on the market since young bulls produce less semen than older animals; therefore, more animals are required to meet revenue goals.

The introduction of genomic testing has coincided with a less desirable outcome: increased inbreeding, the degree to which a dairy bull is related to the rest of the dairy cattle population. We focus our analysis on Holstein cattle, the dominant breed in the US dairy industry, representing roughly 90\% of the US dairy herd and approximately 86\% of bulls marketed by NAAB members during our sample period. Figure \ref{fig:tpi} shows the average inbreeding rate of Holstein dairy bulls on the market alongside the total productivity index (TPI), a measure of overall genetic performance value\footnote{TPI is an economic value-weighted average of a set of $m$ genetic traits: $H=\sum_{j=1}^{m}p_{j}g_{j}$, where $p_{j}$ and $g_{j}$ are the economic values and breeding values of trait $j$ \citep{Melton_Heady_Willham1979}.}.

Inbreeding is an inherent feature of pure-bred selection programs: concentrating genetic material from high-performing ancestors is a deliberate strategy to fix beneficial traits related to production, health, and fertility. However, excessive inbreeding, beyond what is individually or socially optimal, incurs costs from inbreeding depression, depletes genetic diversity, and increases the risk of recessive disease alleles becoming expressed in the population. Many animal scientists worry that inbreeding in Holsteins has reached a level that critically endangers the breed and, by extension, the dairy industry \citep{Cole2023,makanjuola_effect_2020}.

In this paper, we examine whether introducing genomic testing increased inbreeding by incentivizing even more intensive selection of a small group of already popular, ``elite'' bulls.  Our hypothesis first derives from the observations of many animal scientists \citep{scott_how_2021,howard_invited_2017,Cole2019,makanjuola_effect_2020} that selection of a small group of bulls intensified after genomic testing arrived on the market in 2009. Our hypothesis also draws on the economics literature on ``superstars'' pioneered by \citet{rosen_economics_1981} and \citet{adler1985stardom}, which observes that a market can be dominated by an elite group when high and low quality are poor substitutes and the marginal cost of expansion is small. Our work specifically applies the logic of \citet{adler1985stardom} to observe that, even if two bulls are of roughly the same quality, the more popular one will be selected more because of positive network externalities. Implicitly, these theoretical models predict that any technology that makes quality \textit{easier} to discern will increase concentration for superstars and lead to more bulls being discovered and elevated. In our application, genomic testing made it easier to distinguish bulls in the market and, so the theory predicts, increased use of superstars.

To test our hypothesis, we use bull evaluations from 2001 to 2020, before and after genomically tested bulls arrived in 2009, and test whether bulls with similar genetic merit but different popularity had different rates of inbreeding after 2010. Since well-known bulls are likely to be high-quality, we construct a set of counterfactual bulls comparable to popular genetic families in terms of productivity using propensity score matching. We then compare inbreeding rates in popular families with those in our counterfactual families before and after the introduction of genomically tested bulls in 2010. Isolating the contribution of ``popularity'' to increased rates of inbreeding in the industry tests whether any bull with high quality was able to rise to the top after genomic testing or, as \citet{adler1985stardom} predicts, whether the superstars became even more used than before.

We find that superstar dairy bull lines became more inbred after the introduction of genomic testing than in our counterfactual. 
After 2010, the inbreeding rate of the most popular lines increased by 1.2-3.5 percentage points relative to our control group, representing about a 50\% increase over pre-2010 rates. We also find no statistically significant difference in inbreeding rates across our groups before genomic testing was introduced. Using cost estimates from the Council on Dairy Cattle Breeding (CDCB), a back-of-the-envelope calculation implies that increased inbreeding in popular lines cost on the order of USD~1-3~billion over 2012--2019, depending on the share of the national herd descended from those lines (Appendix~\ref{sec:cost}).

This article fills a critical gap in the literature on livestock markets and animal breeder behavior. Few economics papers analyze animal breeders' strategic actions, and even fewer examine how those actions may impose costs on the industry.  Early modeling of animal breeders' decisions dates back to studies such as \citet{ladd1978}, \citet{Kerr1984}, and \citet{Melton1994}, which use hedonic regression and linear programming to determine the implicit market value of different livestock traits. \citet{Richards1996} and \citet{Schroeder1992} apply this method to dairy bulls to determine the economic value of each dairy bull trait implied by the results of a hedonic regression. Thus far, the economics literature has not explored the dynamics of elite lines in dairy breeding or their impact on negative outcomes like inbreeding.

Our article also draws inspiration from the literature on superstars in markets first established by \citet{rosen_economics_1981}. The theoretical work of \citet{rosen_economics_1981} and \citet{adler1985stardom} has since spawned an empirical literature which aims to distinguish whether superstars are simply the ones with the best quality (Rosen's theory) or whether the existing popularity of a superstar helps them rise independent of quality (Adler's theory) \citep{gergaud_untalented_2021,franck_talent_2012,filimon2011test}. Our application has a significant advantage over other studies: quality is well defined in the bull market. We also build on this literature in two ways. First, we examine a case where information technology makes quality easier to discern, which in theory increases concentration on superstars. Second, we examine a case where greater focus on superstars harms the industry by eroding genetic diversity, which is necessary for future genetic improvement \citep{makanjuola_effect_2020,scott_how_2021,Cole2023}.


% Even without explicit externalities, individual profit-maximizing firms can find themselves in situations where coordination would improve product quality, even though coordination is not individually rational. Our paper is the first to explore such a coordination problem in the market for dairy cattle genetics. In the short run, breeders are incentivized to increase inbreeding by breeding cattle from popular lines, which may boost genetic improvement and capture more market share. However, inbreeding expends a type of resource, genetic diversity, that is needed to keep genetic improvement growing over time. Yet it is individually rational for firms to breed more than is socially optimal to maintain their market share by producing the highest-quality bulls \citep{Cole2019}. Welfare loss occurs because maintaining a firm's genetic diversity benefits the entire industry, as other firms can use it to restore their own diversity. However, an individual firm does not consider this benefit, leading to a slower rate of genetic improvement in the long run.

Our article highlights a significant ongoing debate in the dairy industry: how to control for the rate of inbreeding in our empirical specification. Before its introduction, it was theorized that genomic testing would \textbf{decrease} inbreeding by allowing breeders to more easily identify productive dairy bulls outside known genetic lines and better distinguish relatives \citep{Daetwyler2007,de2011effects}. Our work adds something important to this debate by helping explain why precisely the opposite happened. While many animal scientists have mentioned this in passing, no one has demonstrated precisely how superstar dynamics can worsen inbreeding in the dairy bull market. Rather than letting the best bulls rise to the top, our work shows that existing popularity prioritizes insiders and prevents new genetic sources from entering. The erosion of the dairy genetic pool is similar to the erosion of ``local culture'' that \citet{adler_chapter_2006} emphasizes, and, in this case, the dairy industry would feel the loss of genetic diversity strongly and financially in the long run.

In our conceptual framework, we explore how genomic testing increases the returns to inbreeding and likely reduces genetic diversity across the industry. We support this theory by comparing the inbreeding rates of groups of bulls that are similar in genetic traits but differ in popularity before and after genomic testing. If genomic testing increased the discovery of less popular lines, we should expect no difference in the inbreeding rates of these groups, since animal breeders would have selected them solely on genetic traits. Instead, we find that genetic lines already popular before genomic testing experienced an increase in their average inbreeding rate, thereby damaging the dairy sector.

Studies estimate that the Holstein breed has an unsustainable level of inbreeding, with an ``effective population'' of between 43 and 66; 50 is commonly assumed to be a critical level below which the breed will lose resilience \citep{makanjuoal_effect_2020}. Moreover, the lower the genetic diversity, the greater the risk that unknown recessive traits will significantly damage genetic lines \citep{Cole2011, Lozada2024}. At worst, the population can reach the maximum possible increase in genetic trait values, stalling genetic improvement. Future work in economics should examine how policy might address market frictions and incentivize breeders to outcross, thereby mitigating the impacts of inbreeding.

\section{Conceptual Framework\label{sec:concept_frame}}
\subsection{Dairy Bulls as a Superstar Market}
In the dairy bull market, breeders mate bulls and cows to produce new bulls, which thousands of dairy farmers can use by extracting semen for artificial insemination. Each bull on the market possesses a set of genetic traits that tell dairy farmers how much of a given attribute the bull will pass on to its offspring. For example, each dairy bull has a number for milk yield, which is a prediction of how many more pounds of milk the offspring of the bull is likely to produce compared to a baseline birth cohort.
Similar traits exist for health, reproduction, and longevity. To help dairy farmers choose bulls with the highest values of this trait, the USDA combines these into a selection index, called Net Merit, which weights each trait by a profitability weight calculated by the USDA. Dairy farmers use Net Merit extensively to rank dairy bulls, as does the similar Total Performance Index (TPI) published by the breed association Holstein USA \citep{hutchins2026market,VanRaden2021}.

\citet{rosen_economics_1981} mentions two important characteristics of superstar markets, and both of them are arguably present in the dairy bull market. The first of Rosen's conditions is that medium- and high-quality products are poor substitutes. For example, several concerts by a mediocre musician are not as enjoyable as one concert from a high-quality musician. In dairy, this is equivalent to the fact that using several mediocre bulls is not equivalent to using a high-quality bull. While a high-quality bull will permanently raise the genetic quality of a farm's herd in subsequent generations, using several mediocre bulls would risk stagnating genetic progress \citep{hutchins_role_2024}.

The second condition is that, in the superstar market, the resources needed to produce the good do not scale with the number of people served. \citet{rosen_economics_1981} again uses musicians as an example, pointing out that an artist records a song once and can reproduce it at very low cost by selling records. Because one dairy bull can produce millions of semen samples for sale, this also holds in the dairy bull market. The breeder pays a fixed cost to breed the bull but can then sell its service to millions of people at a relatively low marginal cost.

Most importantly, empirical evidence shows that the Holstein dairy bull market is highly concentrated in certain family lines. \citet{Yue2015} find that Holstein family lines have narrowed so much over the past 50 years that practically all Holstein dairy bulls can be traced back to three bulls from the 1960s. The seeming benefits of this focus on a few elite genetic lines can also be empirically verified: since the 1950s, cow milk yield in the US has grown at a linear rate \citep{Hutchins_Hueth2023}. It is commonly claimed that about half of this growth is attributable to dairy breeders' efforts \citep{cdcbimpact}.

The downside of this strategy has been a gradual increase in inbreeding in Holstein cattle \citep{young1996estimates}. Inbreeding first damages the industry because, as inbreeding increases, gains in genetic traits decline, a phenomenon known as ``inbreeding depression'' \citep{charlesworth2009genetics}. The second way inbreeding damages the industry is longer-term but potentially more costly. As the genetic pool continues to narrow, the probability of passing along dangerous recessive traits grows \citep{charlesworth2009genetics}. In one case, one of the three founding bulls that \citet{Yue2015} mentions, named \textit{Pawnee Farm Arlinda Chief} carried a recessive trait which led to 500,000 spontaneous abortions and nearly 420 million dollars in losses because of a damaging disease that was passed to his offspring \citep{Adams2016}.

Summarizing, the dairy bull market has all the hallmarks of a classic superstar market. However, it differs from most applications in the superstar literature for two reasons. First, whereas most of the literature struggles to identify a salient measure of quality, this market clearly defines quality and has measurable benefits (e.g., milk yield). At the same time, the increased concentration in this superstar market also has a measurable negative side effect (e.g., inbreeding depression). Because a breed's genetic pool is a shared, common-pool resource, dairy breeders within a breed like Holstein may narrow it to their collective detriment in the long run.

\subsection{The Impact of Genomic Testing}
Genomic testing, introduced to the industry around 2008, is a technology that drastically changed the market for at least two reasons. First, genomic testing allowed dairy breeders to identify specific genes associated with desirable traits like milk yield and fertility and select animals with those genes. This made quality predictions more accurate than before, so breeders could select bulls more confidently at young ages without first observing their offspring \citep{vanraden2009invited}. Second, because ``proving'' a bull now only requires a DNA sample of the dairy bull as soon as it is born, more bulls could be screened for positive genetic traits. Because the method does not require waiting for offspring data, bulls could also be marketed at younger and younger ages (see Section \ref{sec:data_description}). Both lead to genetic gain in dairy cattle of roughly double the rate after genomic testing \citep{garciaruiz2016}.

Before the introduction of genomics, researchers theorized that genomic testing would reduce inbreeding. \citet{Daetwyler2007} points out that previous methods of selecting bulls to breed often incidentally cross-bred relatives because siblings could not be properly distinguished from each other. Genomic testing avoids this by examining each animal's DNA, which reduces the likelihood of mixing sibling lines. Examining an animal's genome for select traits also makes it possible to discover more diverse, high-value lines \citep{howard_invited_2017,scott_how_2021}. Using a simulation, \citet{de2011effects} shows how a well-managed genomic breeding program with a large enough initial pool of sires can reduce inbreeding and increase genetic gain.

Yet genomic testing is now widely associated with increased inbreeding \citep{makanjuola_effect_2020,scott_how_2021}. The dairy science literature points to at least one biological reason: the region of the genome associated with desirable traits like milk production ended up bringing in more genetic variation than expected. As a result, the cow population started sharing more of the same genes than were originally selected on, a phenomenon known as ``hitchhiking'' in the animal science field \citep{makanjuola_effect_2020}.

Beyond the purely biological, blame has also been placed on dairy breeders for overselecting from a small pool of ``elite'' sires \citep{howard_invited_2017,scott_how_2021,Cole2023}.
\citet{miglior_genetic_2014} found that, while more bulls were genotyped after genomic testing was adopted, roughly the same number of bulls were responsible for making the new sires. \citet{howard_invited_2017} theorizes that, because the industry is ``dispersed,'' competition drives breeders to chase short-term gains at the expense of long-term damage via inbreeding. Yet these papers do not explain why genomic testing, a technology that enables better discovery of new genetics, has incentivized breeders to explore even less than before.

In this paper, we hypothesize that the superstar aspects of the dairy bull market predict that already popular bulls will get even more market share after genomic testing. We draw on two insights from the superstar literature in economics. First, when quality becomes easier to discern, market concentration can increase toward the highest-quality stars. When quality is hard to discern, stars similar to, but below, the top stars can still capture market share \citep{rosen_economics_1981}. If quality becomes easier to discern, market share will shift to the top performers \citep{macdonald_economics_1988}.

The second insight is from \citet{adler1985stardom}: even when two stars are of the same quality, the more popular star will beat out the less popular one. \citet{adler1985stardom} hypothesizes that this is because the bigger the ``fanbase'' is, the lower the cost is of gathering information (or what Adler calls ``consumption capital''). Thus, the best do not necessarily rise to the top, and the market will concentrate in favor of whoever is already popular. In the case of dairy bulls, genomic testing favors already popular bulls because the test is the most accurate for those already closely related to the reference population. As a result, genomic testing gives already popular lines advantages they would not have under the prior system of testing only offspring.

We test our hypothesis on these data by comparing inbreeding rates, essentially a proxy for market concentration of the dominant genetics, of popular bulls to less popular bulls of similar quality before and after genomic testing. If our hypothesis is correct, we would see popular bulls become more inbred after genomic testing was adopted in the market (around 2009) than their less popular but similar-quality counterparts. This would be the first empirical evidence supporting observations in the animal science literature that genomic testing inadvertently increased inbreeding by increasing rewards for selecting an already popular group.

\section{Data Description\label{sec:data_description}}
To answer our research question, we use data on dairy bull traits and lineages, including each bull's inbreeding rate. Dairy bull traits in the US are calculated using data on dairy bull offspring producing milk on US dairy farms. The National Dairy Herd Improvement Program (NDHIP) collects, manages, and analyzes data from dairy farms that are members of Dairy Herd Improvement Associations to assess the bull's contribution to the productivity of their offspring \citep{Hutchins_Hueth2023}. The raw data collected from farms are used to estimate a statistical model that predicts an animal's genetic contribution as a sire (or dam) after controlling for environmental variables such as herd, age, pedigree, and season effects. The predicted values for each trait from this model are commonly called the \textbf{predicted transmitting ability} (PTA) of a trait.

The primary data for our study are from the National Association of Animal Breeders (NAAB), a trade organization representing all major livestock genetics companies that sell dairy bulls in the US. We restrict our sample to Holstein bulls, the dominant breed in the US dairy herd, for whom the NAAB data is most comprehensive. Dairy bull traits for all bulls sold by members are posted three times a year at the same time that each bull's predicted genetic traits are calculated and posted publicly by the Council on Dairy Cattle Breeding (formerly by the USDA) \citep{Hutchins_Hueth2023}. Our data comes from the NAAB's published lists from 2001 to 2020 and represents over 15,400 dairy bulls, both foreign and domestic, sold by its members during this period. The Association represents 95\% of the livestock genetics companies and cooperatives in the US and Canada,\footnote{\url{https://www.naab-css.org/history}} making these data broadly representative of what is sold to US dairy farmers.

The NAAB lists also contain lineage information, which we use to track a bull's descendants. Every animal born in the USA or Canada is assigned a unique ID that includes information about its breed and country of birth. The NAAB data include these IDs for the bull and its two parents, allowing us to build a genetic relationship network. 

To measure inbreeding we use the Pedigree\textbf{inbreeding coefficient}, $F_{X}$ \citep{Wright1922}, as defined below:

\begin{equation}
        F_{X}=\sum_{ca=1}^{K}\left(\dfrac{1}{2}\right)^{n_{1}+n_{2}+1}(1+F_{ca})\label{eq:inb}
\end{equation}

where $ca$ denotes the common ancestor, $n_{1}$ and $n_{2}$ are the numbers of generations separating the common ancestor $c_a$ from the sire and the dam, respectively, and $F_{ca}$ is the inbreeding of the common ancestor. 
A bull produced by crossing lines with similar genetics inherits a larger share of identical-by-descent alleles, raising $F_{X}$.
A rising average $F_{X}$ within a line is a direct signal of the decline in that line's genetic diversity.

In practice, the Council on Dairy Cattle Breeding (CDCB) calculates this using pedigree data collected through the National Dairy Herd Improvement Program (NDHIP) and reports it each time a bull's genetic evaluations are published, three times per year. The inbreeding coefficient used in our empirical analysis is drawn from the NAAB dataset, which reports the CDCB-calculated inbreeding coefficient for each bull at the time of its first release. This figure is calculated using the full national pedigree database and therefore represents the entire US Holstein population served by NAAB members, rather than a regional or membership-restricted subsample.

Figure \ref{fig:tpi}, by contrast, uses the population-average inbreeding rate reported by the Canadian Dairy Network (CDN) for illustrative purposes only, to show the industry-wide trend in inbreeding alongside productivity growth. The CDN reports a population-average inbreeding coefficient calculated using the same \cite{Wright1922} methodology applied to the Canadian Holstein pedigree, and trends in Canadian and US Holstein inbreeding rates are closely comparable given the high degree of genetic exchange between the two countries' breeding programs. We do not use this figure in any of our empirical estimations, which rely exclusively on NAAB-sourced inbreeding coefficients.

The genomic testing program began in 2008, and the first genomically tested bulls had their evaluations published in January 2009. \citet{garciaruiz2016} finds that, after 2010, the generation interval decreased significantly by about four years. 
Using the list of bulls that are actively sold by members of the National Association of Animal Breeders, we can see in Figure \ref{fig:gen_int} that the new genomically proven bulls, first listed in 2010, have an average age of a little less than two years old when they are first released, versus around five years for daughter-proven bulls.

\section{Empirical Framework\label{sec:emp_frame}}
In our empirical approach, we compare lines with similar genetic quality but differing pre-existing popularity, measured by the number of sons before genomic testing. 
To isolate the role of the existing popularity of genetics, we construct a counterfactual sample with the same genetic traits but from a less popular line. 
Specifically, we use propensity score matching \citep{Smith2005} to define a control group that resembles the treatment in all observable characteristics, in this case, the genetic traits.
The assumption of propensity score matching, matching on observables, is more likely to hold for dairy bulls compared to other applications since we observe all of the same information in the NAAB data that dairy farmers observe when making their decisions.

We define a \textbf{line} as the set of direct (male) descendants from a particular bull. 
We also define a ``popular'' bull as a bull whose number of sons is in the 95th percentile of the distribution of offspring. 
To construct the line, we start by considering sires born between 1998 and 2004, as the ``founders'' of each line. Our definition depends critically on when the founders of such lines were born; if we choose too early a date, lines are hard to distinguish because all bulls are related to some degree. 
After identifying the sires on which to build lines, we recursively build their pedigrees by matching each bull to its sire (father) until we reach the latest animal in the sample. 
Figure \ref{fig:treat_control} in the appendix summarizes the sample selection mechanism; the treatment and control groups comprise the descendants of two groups: popular bulls (treatment) and matched bulls (control).

Breeders are under high competitive pressure to supply bulls with the highest PTAs. However, the best way to do so is by linebreeding a family of bulls so that the relative distance to an ancestor remains constant across generations. \cite{Steyn2022} and \cite{Steyn2023} show that younger sires capture a more significant share of present-day population genetic variation; hence, genotypes of older sires can be discarded from (genomic) evaluations. As generations begin to accumulate, the contribution of a particular sire decreases (on average) exponentially, so we can disregard the impact of distant ancestors. 

% The reason behind this is that bulls are advertised only with their sire and grandsire; any ancestry beyond that is not taken into account by dairy farmers. If we choose a later date, both treatment and control groups will have little difference in inbreeding rates because there is not enough time for the control group lines to develop. 

% We want to measure the causal effect of introducing genomic testing in 2009 on the average inbreeding in extant lines; the first problem we must tackle is the nature of a valid comparison group. The introduction of genomic testing caused animal breeders to supply more animals from well-known lines, that is, descended from superstar sires; our control group must be the lines descended from other non-superstar sires. 

The first step is to match the founders of such lines with other sires that did not father as many sons but had comparable genetic traits. Second, we identify all descendants from both groups and compare how their inbreeding rates evolved over time. The idea behind this procedure is that when those bulls were in the market, any reasonably similar sire could have been chosen but was not, and thus, can be used to construct a counterfactual line. To do so, we use a propensity score matching estimator, let $p(X)=\Pr(T_{j} = 1 | \mathbf{X})$ where $T$ is a dummy equal to 1 if sire $j$'s offspring is on the 95$^{th}$ percentile of the distribution and $X_{j}$ be a vector comprised of the following:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Predicted Transmitting Ability for production traits (milk, fat, and protein), health traits (mastitis, metritis, productive life), and reproductive traits (daughter pregnancy rate, early first calving, gestation length).
    \item Dummies for the absence of certain recessive genetic disorders \citep{sire_workbbok2021} such as \textit{brachyspina}\footnote{A lethal genetic disease that causes spontaneous abortions.}, complex vertebral malformation, cholesterol deficiency, and mule foot.
    \item Dummies for the presence of genes associated with specific qualitative traits (polled, or red coat color).
    \item Dummies for the presence of haplotypes (specific sequences of DNA at different locations in the chromosome) that affect fertility \citep{haplotypes2018} when present in homozygous form (likely to be expressed when two carrier animals are mated).
\end{enumerate}

We then estimate the propensity score \citep[p.~873]{Cameron_Trivedi2005}, the probability of being a popular bull given the level of PTAs and the full set of variables listed above. The validity of our method relies on a series of assumptions \citep{Heckman1997}:

\medskip
\textbf{Assumption 1} (Stable Unit Treatment Value Assumption): $(inb^{treatment}, inb^{control}) \perp\!\!\!\perp T/\mathbf{X}$.

SUTVA requires two conditions to hold \citep{Imbens2015}. First, the potential outcomes of one unit should not be affected by the treatment assigned to other units (no interference). Second, there should be only one version of each treatment level (no hidden versions of treatment).

The second condition is plausibly satisfied in our setting: genomic testing was introduced as a single, industry-wide technological change in 2008, and all firms faced the same testing technology. No meaningfully different "versions" of genomic testing would affect some lines differently from others through the treatment itself.

The first condition, however, warrants more careful discussion. Our conceptual framework explicitly models a coordination failure in which one firm's inbreeding decisions affect the genetic diversity available to other firms for outcrossing. In this sense, SUTVA may be partially violated by construction: if popular lines become more inbred after genomic testing, this mechanically reduces the diversity available to other lines and may affect their inbreeding rates as well. A strict reading of the no-interference assumption would therefore be difficult to defend in this setting.

We address this concern in two ways. First, our identification strategy focuses on the differential effect of genomic testing on popular versus non-popular lines, rather than on the absolute level of inbreeding in either group. Cross-line spillovers would need to affect popular and non-popular lines asymmetrically and in a way unrelated to their pre-existing popularity to bias our estimates. Second, the matching procedure ensures that treatment and control founders are comparable in all observable genetic traits before genomic testing, so any spillovers that affect both groups symmetrically will be differenced out in our estimator. We therefore interpret our estimates as the differential causal effect of genomic testing on inbreeding in popular lines relative to comparable non-popular lines, rather than as an absolute treatment effect on inbreeding for the industry as a whole.

Figure \ref{fig:inb_dist} shows the extent to which the inbreeding rate distribution of our matched sample resembles that of the treatment group. Unlike unmatched sires, our matched sires have similar inbreeding distributions conditional on the set of genetic traits. In a similar fashion, Figure \ref{fig:love_plot} shows the standardized mean differences of all variables used to estimate the propensity score. Balance is achieved for several variables, notably for all haplotype dummies and several PTAs.

Each year, these bulls will have a new set of descendants being born, each with its own inbreeding rate and genetic traits; if our hypothesis holds, the inbreeding rates of the descendants of popular bulls should be significantly higher than those of non-popular descendants.\footnote{In Appendix \ref{app:estimator}, we discuss three more assumptions that must hold for our estimator to be valid, including parallel trends, no anticipatory effects, and asymptotic convergence.}

Let $j=1,\dots, J$ be the set of lines, defined as the set of direct descendants from a single male ancestor. Each line is comprised of $i=1,\dots,n_{j}$ bulls born in year $t$ and belongs to firm $k=1,\dots, K$. \begin{equation*}
    inb_{ijkt}=treat_{j}\times inb_{ijkt}^{treat}+(1-treat_{j})\times inb_{ijkt}^{control}
\end{equation*}

Where $inb_{ijkt}$ is the inbreeding rate of bull $i$ from line $j$, which was marketed by firm $k$ and was born in year $t$. To measure the effects of genomic selection on inbreeding, we estimate the difference-in-differences model:

\begin{align}
    inb_{ijkt} &= treat_{j}+post_{t}+(treat_{j}\times post_{t})\beta+\varepsilon_{ijkt}\tag{Model 1}\\
    inb_{ijkt} &= treat_{j}+treat_{j}\times\sum_{\substack{t=2005\\t \neq 2009}}^{2019}\beta_{t}\mathbf{I}(year=t)+\alpha_{t}+\varepsilon_{ijkt}\tag{Model 2}\\
    inb_{ijkt} &= treat_{j}+treat_{j}\times\sum_{\substack{t=2005\\t \neq 2009}}^{2019}\beta_{t}\mathbf{I}(year=t)+\alpha_{t}+\alpha_{k}+\mathbf{X}_{it}\delta+\varepsilon_{ijkt}\tag{Model 3}\\
    inb_{ijkt} &= treat_{j}+treat_{j}\times\sum_{\substack{t=2005\\t \neq 2009}}^{2019}\beta_{t}\mathbf{I}(year=t)+\alpha_{t}+\alpha_{k}+\mathbf{X}_{it}\delta+\left(\mathbf{X}_{it}\times post_{t}\right)\rho+\varepsilon_{ijkt}\tag{Model 4}
    \end{align}

Model (2) excludes covariates (PTAs) and decomposes the ATT by year-of-birth (yob) cohort. Model (3) incorporates the bull's genetic traits ($\mathbf{X}_{it}$) and firm-level fixed effects, and Model (4) includes interactions of genetic traits and the post-treatment dummy. We include the last specification to account for trait-level trends. We also know that the treatment affects both inbreeding rates and PTAs, but we do not have a structural model of their impact \citep{Lewbel2019}, so we include them nonlinearly at both levels and in interactions to reduce uncertainty about the functional form of our controls.

\section{Results\label{sec:results}}

Figure \ref{fig:inb_match} shows the treatment and control groups' average inbreeding rates and confidence intervals across years. Genomic selection did not immediately increase inbreeding rates; both curves began to diverge substantially after 2011. Inbreeding rates then began to increase two years later, and both curves diverged significantly from 2012 onward. Because genomic testing was adopted essentially industry-wide after 2010 (Figure \ref{fig:percent_supply}), no valid non-adopting counterfactual is available, and our matched design identifies the \emph{differential} effect of genomic testing on inbreeding in popular lines relative to trait-matched, less-popular lines, not its aggregate effect. The control group's own upward trend after 2015 is consistent with genomic testing diffusing into less-popular lines as well; our estimates capture only the \emph{additional} inbreeding concentrated in popular lines, and $\hat{\beta}_{t}$ should be read as a lower bound on genomic testing's total effect on industry-wide inbreeding.

\section{Discussion\label{sec:discussion}}
This article investigates the consequences of genomic selection on the US dairy industry. On the one hand, this new technology significantly improved productivity. On the other hand, we find evidence that it increased the average inbreeding rate of all bulls in the market. As genomic testing increased the rate of genetic improvement, it appeared to exert competitive pressure on animal breeders to select from popular lines with high gains. By not crossing enough with other lines, the inbreeding rate increases, dampening future productivity gains by depleting genetic diversity over the long term. As the average inbreeding rate increases, each new generation of animals faces a higher risk of inbreeding depression and of unpredictable recessive genetic diseases.

The increase in inbreeding we document is not necessarily evidence of harmful behavior by individual breeders. As the referee rightly notes, some degree of inbreeding is both inevitable and strategically rational in purebred selection programs, and its net effects depend heavily on the monitoring and selection practices in place. Our concern is specifically whether genomic testing has pushed the industry beyond the socially optimal level of inbreeding, a question that our empirical results address only indirectly. A full welfare analysis would require knowledge of the social optimum $d^{*}$, which is outside the scope of this paper. The USDA publishes a profitability index called Net Merit that estimates a given dairy bull's profitability contribution based on its genetic traits. Even after correcting for inbreeding depression, we estimate that the index increased by 275 USD per animal relative to what it might have been if genetic traits had not increased after 2010.\footnote{Estimated as the 2011--2020 average deviation of the Net Merit index from an ARIMA projection fit to its 1985--2009 values, i.e.\ the per-animal gain relative to the pre-genomic-testing trend.}

The highest per-animal cost of inbreeding in our findings is about 140 USD, compared with lines that are not popular. These costs are difficult to compare since one is relative to a control group and the other is not.\footnote{See Appendix A for more details on our calculations.} Regardless, it appears that the net benefits of genomic testing are positive in the short term. This short-run comparison omits the direct cost of genomic testing itself (genotyping expenditure and any associated replacement or culling losses which our data do not measure). A full benefit--cost analysis of genomic testing is outside this paper's scope. We note, however, that the cumulative discounted genotyping cost over our window is of a substantially smaller order of magnitude than the estimated productivity benefit, so its omission narrows but does not reverse the net-positive result. 

While genomic testing clearly offers short-term gains, the actual welfare effects are harder to discern. As we explore in our conceptual framework, welfare may still fall relative to the counterfactual of no genomic testing. However, our paper does not aim to examine such a counterfactual; instead, it focuses on the relative increase in inbreeding among popular lines. This is because reducing genetic diversity through inbreeding increases short-run genetic gain at the cost of steadier long-run productivity growth. At its worst, excessive inbreeding can lead to population collapse, leaving no genetic diversity to sustain continued production.

Because dairy cattle breeders are not incentivized to cross-breed in the current market, this suggests a role for the government or an industry group to help address the coordination problem. Currently, the Holstein Association of the United States adjusts genetic trait values based on inbreeding levels to penalize breeders who select bulls with high inbreeding, since such animals carry higher reproductive costs. Yet, \citet{Cole2023} points out that many of the most inbred bulls have such high levels of traits that the downgrading will not temper the demand for them enough. Another option would be to give dairy farmers more information about the dangers of inbreeding to reduce demand for the most inbred lines \citep{Cole2023}.

% Similar coordination problems have been confronted in the application of pesticides and the use of Bt-resistant crops by farmers.
% In the case of Bt-resistant crops, the US EPA and many countries adopted a command-and-control strategy that mandates farmers plant some area to non-Bt-resistant crops \citep{huang2011success}.
% This amounts to a tax, in the form of a yield penalty, on farmers to force them to internalize the costs of their actions.
% Whether such a strategy would work for dairy cattle breeding would depend on some key factors.
% Namely, how logistically might a government or organization impose a similar penalty on dairy cattle breeders to steer them away from inbreeding?
More research is needed in this area of agricultural economics, as the coordination problems in dairy cattle breeding have not yet been examined in the field. Future work could make a significant contribution by expanding our conceptual framework to understand the socially optimal level of inbreeding and the circumstances under which a technology like genomic testing actually deepens the welfare losses from inbreeding. A full cost-benefit analysis of genomic testing is, unfortunately, outside the scope of this paper. Yet, studying increases in inbreeding matters for a safe, globally resilient dairy sector and for ensuring that US dairy cattle breeders continue to sustainably raise dairy cattle productivity.

\section{Genomic Selection Cost Appraisal\label{sec:cost}}
Using published estimates of inbreeding costs, we can assign a monetary value to increases in inbreeding rates. The Council of Dairy Cattle Breeding (CDCB) estimates that each additional percentage of inbreeding is associated with a 40.11 USD reduction in income from dairy product sales. The total cost depends on the average inbreeding rate we consider.
 
We can use the coefficients from Model (4), our preferred specification, as the conditional difference in inbreeding rates between the control and treatment groups. In other words, we can assign monetary values to the Average Treatment on the Treated (ATT) in our sample. We cannot extrapolate this result to the entire set of bulls, since we did not choose our sample at random. The per-animal average increased cost in year $t$ is equal to:

\begin{equation}
    \overline{Cost}_{t} = 40.11\times\hat{\beta}_{t}
\end{equation}

Because our ATT measures the \emph{differential} cost of inbreeding in popular lines relative to matched controls (Section \ref{sec:emp_frame}), we set $N_{t}$ equal to the number of registered descendants of popular founder lines born in year $t$, the treated population to which the ATT applies, rather than the total US Holstein population. This yields a partial accounting of the inbreeding costs induced by genomic testing: it excludes the concurrent rise in inbreeding among non-popular lines (Figure \ref{fig:inb_match}), which our design differences out. The resulting figure is best understood as the cost of the popularity-specific inbreeding premium, and a conservative component of the industry's total inbreeding cost. Because we do not directly observe the treated (popular-line) share of the national herd, we report the cost across a range of assumed shares. Finally, we calculate the net present value using a discount rate ($r$) equal to 4.5\%:

\begin{eqnarray*}
NPV &=& \sum_{t = 2012}^{2019}\dfrac{1}{(1+r)^{t-2012}}N_{t}\times \overline{Cost}_{t}\\
&=& \sum_{t = 2012}^{2019}\dfrac{1}{(1+r)^{t-2012}}N_{t}\times\left(40.11\times \hat{\beta}_{t}\right)\\
\end{eqnarray*}

Table \ref{tab:cost_npv} reports the resulting NPV (2012 base year). Applied only to the popular-line descendants we observe, the cost is a firm floor of about USD~0.4~million and involves no extrapolation. Scaling the same per-animal differential to the fraction of the national Holstein herd descended from popular lines yields USD~1.3-3.0~billion at a 4.5\% discount rate for shares between 30\% and 70\%. \cite{Wuepper2023} document that farmers in North America and Western Europe apply higher discount rates than the market rate, around 23\% per annum; at that rate the same figures fall to roughly USD~0.6-1.4~billion. These magnitudes reflect the costs on one side of a heterogeneous market and should be read as illustrative, assuming a given share.

\section{Benefits from Genomic Selection\label{sec:benefit}} 
While genomic testing increased inbreeding, it also increased milk production and, in turn, profits for the dairy industry.

We can calculate the benefits of introducing genomic selection in our sample using an index called \textbf{Net Merit}, which measures net profit over the lifetime of the average daughter of the animal, expressed in U.S. dollars. Net merit is calculated by multiplying the values of genetic traits by a set of dollar values intended to capture the ``market value'' of all traits.\footnote{Table \ref{tab:net_merit} shows what are the magnitudes of such weights \citep{VanRaden2021}. Note that the calculation involves the usage of subindexes for health and reproduction traits, as well as an estimation of residual feed intake per animal.}

Published values of Net Merit are already adjusted for inbreeding; the increase in quality will exceed the losses from inbreeding depression. A more accurate estimate of the benefits requires more information than we have; we need expected future inbreeding for all animals in our sample. We do not have the information required to perform such a calculation.\footnote{Let $g_{k}$ be the reported PTA of trait $k$ from bull $i$ and $\tilde{g}_{ik}$ be the unadjusted PTA, then their relationship will be: $\tilde{g}_{ik}=g_{ik}-\left[(EFI_{i}-F_{i})\hat{\beta}_{k}\right]$, where $EF_{i}$ is the expected future inbreeding and $\hat{\beta}_{k}$ is the regression coefficient from a linear model that relates observed values with phenotypic values.} 

In principle, one could compare the Net Merit of popular-line descendants with that of matched controls to gauge whether the extra genetic gain in popular lines offsets their higher inbreeding cost. In practice, our design does not identify this net welfare effect. The per-year Net Merit differential between popular and control lines is small relative to its sampling variability (driven by the limited size of the matched control group) and switches sign across birth cohorts. A line-clustered bootstrap of the benefit--cost ratio yields a 95\% interval that spans values well below and well above one (equivalently, a net present value whose interval comfortably contains zero), so we do not report a net welfare figure. We therefore restrict our welfare accounting to the inbreeding \emph{cost} of the popularity premium, which is well identified (Appendix~\ref{sec:cost}), and leave estimation of the net welfare effect to future work with larger control samples.

\section{Additional Estimator Assumptions}
\label{app:estimator}
Our causal estimand of interest is the Average Treatment on the Treated (ATT) in the post-intervention period:

\begin{equation*}
    ATT_{post}=E\left[inb_{post}^{treat}-inb_{post}^{control} \middle| T_{j}=1\right]
\end{equation*}

This quantity measures the average causal effect on the treated lines in the post-treatment period \citep{Roth2023}. To correctly identify causal effects, a series of assumptions must hold \citep{Sun2021}:

\medskip
\textbf{Assumption 3} (Parallel trends): 
\begin{equation*}
    E\left[inb_{post}^{control}-inb_{pre}^{control}\middle| T_{j}=1\right] = E\left[inb_{post}^{control}-inb_{pre}^{control} \middle| T_{j}=0\right]
\end{equation*}

We assume that the average inbreeding rates for both groups would have evolved in parallel without introducing genomic selection. This assumption is not testable because we cannot observe untreated inbreeding rates after 2009, but it constrains our model specification to a Two-Way Fixed Effects specification we call Model (1):

\begin{equation}
inb_{ijkt} = treat_{j}+post_{t}+(treat_{j}\times post_{t})\beta+\varepsilon_{ijkt}\tag{Model 1}
\end{equation}

Where $inb_{ijkt}$ is the inbreeding rate of bull $i$ from line $j$, supplied by firm $k$ and born in year $t$; $treat_{j}$ indicates whether line $ j$ is defined as the difference-in-differences in observed outcomes between the pre- or post-2009 period, and $\varepsilon_{ijkt}$ is the error term. Firm fixed effects enter Models 3 and 4, not Model 1. 

\medskip
\textbf{Assumption 4} (No anticipatory effects): $inb_{pre}^{control}=inb_{pre}^{treat}$

This assumption ensures that both groups were comparable before the treatment; inbreeding rates of popular lines did not increase before the introduction of genomic selection; we use matching methods to ensure that this condition holds. 

Under Assumptions 3 and 4, we have that:

\begin{equation}
    ATT_{post}=E\left[inb_{post}^{treat}-inb_{post}^{control} \middle| T_{j}=1\right]-E\left[inb_{pre}^{treat}-inb_{pre}^{control} \middle| T_{j}=0\right]
\end{equation}

The ATT is defined as the difference-in-differences in observed outcomes between the pre- and post-intervention periods. All elements in this equation are observed and, therefore, estimable. Suppose popular bulls had superior traits to our matched control group. In that case, this condition would be violated, and we could not parse out the causal effect of inbreeding from the effect of the bull's genetic superiority. Assumptions 3 and 4 ensure that the OLS estimates $\hat{\beta}$ from Model (2) are consistent and asymptotically valid \citep{Roth2023}.

\medskip
\textbf{Assumption 5}: Let $W_{j}=(inb_{j,pre}, inb_{j,post}, T_{j})^{'}$ be a vector of outcomes and treatment status for unit $i$. Then, we observe a sample of $N$  independent and identically distributed random draws $W_{i}\sim F_{W}(w)$ for a distribution $F$ satisfying parallel trends.

Under Assumptions 3, 4, and 5: $\sqrt{N}(\hat{\beta}-ATT_{post})\overset{D}{\rightarrow} N(0, \sigma^{2})$, then we can consistently estimate the variance $\sigma^{2}$ clustering at the line level \citep{Bertrand2004} asymptotically ($N\rightarrow\infty$ and $T$ fixed).

We can also decompose the variation in treatment time, since each year a certain number of bulls will be born from lines in both groups, each with its own inbreeding rate that can be averaged across the treatment and control groups. Models 2-4 show the complete specification, including leads and lags, for the treated group. These models decompose the estimated ATT relative to the treatment average by birth year. \cite{Sun2021} call this specification ``Dynamic specification.'' However, treatment is not staggered: genomic selection affects all lines in the same year, whereas inbreeding rates evolve independently thereafter. 

This assumption states that an individual's treatment status on any given line is unaffected by others' treatment status. In our setting, the treatment (genomic selection) affects all individuals, so no selection into treatment occurs.

\section{Robustness Checks\label{subsec:robustness}}
\subsection{Alternative Matching Estimators}\label{subsec:altmatch}
 
Our baseline constructs the control group by nearest-neighbor matching on a logit propensity score. To assess sensitivity to this choice, we re-estimated the matched control group under seven alternative estimators: Mahalanobis-distance matching, propensity-score matching with a caliper of $0.2$ standard deviations, one-to-one matching without replacement, optimal pair matching, full matching, inverse-probability weighting (IPW), and entropy balancing. 

Table~\ref{tab:altmatch} reports, for each method, the number of covariates (of 74) achieving an absolute standardized mean difference below $0.1$, the largest remaining difference, and the effective sample size (ESS) of the matched control group.
 
Three of the alternatives are not feasible in our setting, and the reason is instructive. With only 40 treated founders and 74 matching covariates, methods that rely on a high-dimensional distance metric or on exact moment balancing break down. Mahalanobis matching balances only 32 of 74 covariates, as Euclidean distance loses discriminating power in high dimensions. Full matching and entropy balancing achieve worse balance than the baseline while driving the effective control sample toward degeneracy: entropy balancing places essentially all weight on a single control unit (control ESS $\approx 1$) yet still leaves 49 covariates imbalanced, and full matching collapses the control ESS to below nine. We therefore do not report these three methods as robustness checks; their failure reflects the dimensionality of the balancing problem relative to the number of treated units, not a design deficiency.
 
Among the feasible estimators, the results reinforce the baseline. Caliper matching achieves the best balance of any method (67 of 74 covariates below the $0.1$ threshold, improving on the baseline's 56), at the cost of a smaller matched treated group, since the caliper discards superstar founders with no sufficiently close control (treated ESS falls from 40 to 29). Inverse-probability weighting balances 59 of 74 covariates while retaining a comparable control sample (ESS $\approx 62$). In both cases, the balance is at least as good as the baseline without degeneracy.
 
Most importantly, the difference-in-differences estimate is stable across these specifications. Re-estimating Model~(1) on the caliper-matched sample yields a treatment-by-post coefficient of 1.19 percentage points (0.423), and the IPW-weighted specification yields 0.98 (0.458), both of similar magnitude to the baseline estimate of $1.45$ percentage points (0.607) and statistically significant at conventional levels.

Figure~\ref{fig:ev_plot} overlays the event-study coefficients under the baseline, caliper, and IPW specifications; the post-2011 divergence in inbreeding between popular and control lines is present and of similar magnitude in all three. We conclude that our findings are not an artifact of the propensity-score matching estimator.

\subsection{Total number of descendants\label{suvsubsec:progeny}}

In our analysis, we defined a ``popular bull'' as a bull whose total number of descendants was among the 95$^{th}$ percentile of their distribution by 2021. The cutoff point also influences this calculation, since genomic testing expands the supply of bulls, and the size of any line is endogenous to genomic selection. It can be argued that selecting bulls based on the ex-post number of sons is tautological in this context.

Figure \ref{fig:coef_plot_robust} plots the coefficients for the different differences-in-differences specifications, but this time, the number of descendants is calculated only up to 2010, so we assume that genomic selection is just being introduced, and we are trying to guess which lines will be more successful. Coefficients for 2014 and 2015 are now substantially reduced and become insignificant, but the overall result remains unchanged; we still have that the treatment group has notoriously higher inbreeding rates than the control group, as seen in the $2\times 2$ model, where the coefficient is still positive and significant for the entire period, as seen in \ref{tab:table4}.

Since we have the exact date of birth for every animal in the sample, we can also group them by quarter of birth; Figure \ref{fig:coef_plot_quarter} shows the results from Models 2-4, replacing the year with quarter-of-birth interactions with the treatment. This article's results depend heavily on a somewhat arbitrary definition of a ``popular'' bull in terms of line size. What would happen if we changed that from the 95th to another quantile in the line-size distribution? Figure \ref{fig:placebo_plots} shows the coefficient plots for different definitions of ``popular''. 

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/placebo_plots.png}
   \caption{Robustness test: changes in the definition of ``popular''}
    \label{fig:placebo_plots}

If we change the cutoff point from the 95th to the 99th percentile, the sign and magnitude of our coefficients do not change much. Still, the confidence intervals widen significantly because our treatment group comprises such specific animals that the matching algorithm cannot find suitable matches. As a consequence, the significance of the difference-in-differences coefficients decreases significantly. Going in the other direction, we can find marginally better matches for the popular group. However, the control group ended abruptly in 2015 because most of the original group's control lines are now included in the treatment, making the control group short-lived. We conclude that our results depend critically on a set of lines that allow for a good comparison when included in the control group; otherwise, the analysis yields insignificant results.
